AI Voice Agents

How a voice AI agent handles a messy real-world phone call

Demos are clean. Real calls have traffic noise, half-heard numbers, callers who switch to Hindi mid-sentence and people who want a price. Here is what has to happen, step by step, for an agent to get through one.

Every voice AI demo sounds good. A quiet room, a patient caller, a clear question with one answer. The calls a business actually gets are different. The caller is on a two-wheeler. They say a date as "parso" and a budget as "sixty-seventy". They interrupt halfway through a sentence, ask for the manager, and then want to know the price before anything else.

This is a practical breakdown of what a voice agent has to do on a call like that, and the rules we build into every agent so it behaves well when the call goes off script. None of it needs a research lab. It needs careful rules and a lot of testing.

The pipeline in one breath

A phone call to an AI agent passes through five pieces, every turn, in about a second:

Phone lineSpeech to textLanguage model, rules and toolsText to speechBack on the line

Each hop adds delay and each can get something wrong. The speech-to-text step can mishear. The model can say too much. The voice can mispronounce a number. Most of the engineering is about making each step fail safely instead of failing loudly.

Step 1. Hearing through noise

The agent never hears audio. It reads a transcript that a speech system produced from the audio, and that transcript is often wrong in small ways. A name comes out as a different name. "Fifteen" becomes "fifty".

So the agent needs a rule for what to do with a garbled word, and the rule depends on how much the word matters:

  • Small words, meaning still clear: carry on. Never repeat the garbled version back, because that tells the caller you did not understand.
  • Things that matter: an amount, a date or time, a name, an address, which option they chose, or a yes or no to the next step. Here the agent does not guess. It says it could not hear clearly and asks once more.

One wrong guess on a date costs more than one repeated question. That trade-off sits at the heart of a good agent.

Step 2. Short turns, one question at a time

People on a phone cannot scroll back. A paragraph goes over their head, and a list of three questions gets one answer. Every turn the agent speaks should carry one thing, in plain words, with at most one number, and then stop.

Too longTell me what you need help with, since when it has been happening, and whether you have spoken to us before?

RightOkay. Tell me, what do you need help with?

Short turns also make interruptions easier. When the caller starts speaking, the agent stops. If they ask the same thing twice, they did not get it the first time, so the agent says it again in fewer, different words instead of repeating itself like a recording.

Step 3. English, Hindi and Hinglish

A large share of business calls in India move between languages within a sentence. The agent should follow the caller, not force a language on them. That works well today for English, Hindi and Hinglish. Other Indian languages and strong regional accents are improving but are not equally reliable yet, so we only promise them after testing with real callers.

There is a quieter problem too. Whatever the model writes, the voice reads out exactly. So the agent's replies are written for the ear, not the eye:

  • Numbers are written in words, because "10:30" can come out as "ten colon thirty".
  • No lists, brackets or symbols, because the voice stumbles on them.
  • A question ends with a question mark, because that is the only thing that makes the voice lift its pitch.

Step 4. Questions it must not answer

The most dangerous answer an agent can give is a confident wrong one. An invented price or an invented delivery date sounds exactly as believable as a real one. So every agent we build has a short list of things it never makes up: prices, discounts, dates, statuses, policies and client names that were not given to it.

When a caller asks for one of those, the agent gives the approved answer once, for example that the team confirms a fixed price after understanding the requirement, and then goes back to where the call was. It does not say "it depends" and stop.

Making an agent talk is easy now. Making it stay inside its rules on every call is the actual work.

Step 5. The calls that go off script

Most turns need no special handling. A handful of situations come up often enough that each gets its own rule, written down with the reason behind it:

  • They are busy right now. Offer exactly one callback at a time they choose, and end the call warmly. Pushing on loses the lead.
  • "Is this a scam? How did you get my number?" Say plainly where the details came from and who is calling. A clear, honest answer rebuilds trust fastest.
  • They want the manager or the owner. Ask whether that person can speak now, and if not, offer a callback. Qualifying someone who cannot decide wastes both sides' time.
  • They already have a developer. Ask whether there is anything not covered. If not, thank them and end the call. Never criticise the other team.
  • They are upset about the call. Acknowledge it in one line and offer to call another time.
  • Wrong number, a clear no, or abuse. One short polite line, then end the call.

Step 6. Honesty rules that never bend

A few rules apply to every agent regardless of the business it works for:

  • If someone sincerely asks whether they are speaking to a person or an AI, the agent says it is an AI assistant, in one line, and carries on.
  • It never claims to have done something it cannot do, such as "I have sent you the details", because the caller then waits for something that never arrives.
  • It never asks for, accepts or repeats an OTP, a PIN, a password or a card number. If a caller starts reading one out, it stops them. That is exactly what a scam sounds like.

Step 7. What happens after the call

The call is half the job. The other half is what lands in your systems when it ends. Depending on the package, that is a summary of the call, the fields the caller gave (name, need, budget, preferred time) written into a sheet or CRM, a booking on a calendar, a WhatsApp follow-up to the caller, and a transfer or callback task for your team when a person needs to step in.

The test of a good agent is simple: can someone on your team read the log in ten seconds and know exactly what to do next?

How we test before go-live

Before an agent takes a real call, we run it against a written list of hard calls: noise, interruptions, language switches, price questions, the scam question, a caller reading out a card number. We listen to the recordings, change one rule at a time and run the list again. During the build you get a test number every Friday so you can call it yourself.
